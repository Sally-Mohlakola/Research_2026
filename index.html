Can a small network learn how light moves inside a diamond?

My honours project. I tried to replace the light transport inside a diamond with a neural network. It learned, and became more accurate, and the diamonds it rendered matched the real ones less.

The questionwhere it started

Why would a cloth model fail on a diamond?

Soh and Montazeri's neural cloth model learns how light leaves a yarn for each incoming direction. That works because hundreds of fibres fit inside one pixel. A diamond inverts this: a few dozen large facets, each covering thousands of pixels, and where light enters decides which facet it leaves through.

So my question became: can a small neural network replace the light transport inside a diamond, and if not, which of its errors explain why the image differs? I tested three hypotheses.

  • H1The decoder that predicts exits most accurately does not give the image that agrees best with the real diamond.
  • H2Errors grow as light meets more facets inside the diamond, and are worst at the pavilion.
  • H3A network query is faster than tracing, yet a full neural render is neither faster nor as accurate.

Setting. Three procedural cuts (round, pear, step), one studio rig of nine textured area lights, an exact spectral path tracer in Mitsuba 3 as the reference.

Aug – Septhe legacy route

Is it a sampling problem, or the wrong variable?

I started by copying the cloth design directly: gather a radiance map on a diamond mesh and shade the surface with it. It rendered flat. Restoring each bin's spread, after LEAN mapping, gave the right amount of sparkle in the wrong places. A lookup test finally named the problem: grouping paths by direction predicts the exit triangle 6.8% of the time, and grouping them by the triangle light enters through, 19.2%. The missing variable was position.

The aim offset. My first gathers aimed every ray at the diamond's centre. Even 2.68 billion rays filled at most 41 of 64 direction bins. Aiming at random points on a disc filled all 64 with 138,000.

Mid Septemberthe operator

What if one network call replaced the inside of the diamond?

The surface is still traced exactly. Everything after light refracts in is one prediction: does it escape, through which facet, and where and in which direction does it leave?

light enters→network: position, direction, normal, wavelength→escape? facet? exit→light continues

I compared three ways of placing the exit, because they make different bets. One spreads its guess over several exits; the others commit to one.

Training. About a million traced paths per cut, half from the camera and half uniform with the aim offset. Five training seeds per decoder and cut: 45 runs, each to convergence, on the Wits cluster.

Stochastic

Learns a spread of possible exits, a mixture of four Gaussians, and samples one. When unsure, it hedges.

Clone

The baseline: predicts one exit by behavioural cloning. When unsure, it commits.

Deep decoder

A deeper clone with its own encoder. More capacity, still one committed exit.

October45 runs back

Does a more accurate operator give a more accurate diamond?

No. The deterministic decoders pick exit facets more accurately, and their diamonds look more like diamonds. Yet their pixel correlation with the reference is lower on every cut, in 29 of 30 paired training seeds. Drag to compare, switch cut and decoder, or hand the network the true exit facet.

Why correlation? SSIM and FLIP punish Monte Carlo noise. I compare against a noise floor: two independent halves of the reference correlate at 0.94–0.98, the best any method could score.

Octoberreading the failures

Where does the network go wrong?

On long paths. Light that meets two or three facets is predicted well; light that bounces around a lot almost never is. Crossing any internal facet edge sends the light out of a different facet, and a small smooth network can only blend between answers.

The true facet test. Handing the network the correct exit facet brings the contrast back (pear clone 4.90 against 1.44), but correlation stays at 0.05–0.13. The exit is still misplaced inside the facet.

A bug worth findingentry sampling

Why did the underside go dark?

Training rays fired only from the camera never enter through the pavilion. When the pear tumbled and showed its underside, the network had never seen that light. Adding rays from every direction, with the same aim offset that fixed my radiance map, brought escape from 46% back to 100%.

Octoberthe cost

Is it at least faster?

Per decision, yes: 10.5 µs against 31 µs to trace. Per image, no. A neural render takes 345 s against 264 s, because gathering, preparing and decoding the network's batches costs more than the tracing it replaces. At equal render time, the exact tracer is 7–10 times more accurate.

Transfer. Re-pointed at another cut without retraining, an operator keeps coarse brightness from round to pear, and fails outright from pear to step cut (correlation −0.02).

Making the network more accurate made the diamond look more real, and match the real one less.

H1 holds

Operator accuracy does not reach the image: the sharper decoders agree less on every cut.

H2, in part

Errors follow path length. The pavilion only fails when it is missing from training.

H3 holds

Each decision is faster, yet renders are slower and never reach the exact tracer's quality.

What I'd try next

Training that weights each exit by the light it carries, the way the image does; local grid encodings for long paths; and the same study under other lighting.
